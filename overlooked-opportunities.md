## Overview

Higher education research has produced a growing body of evidence pointing to strategies that demonstrably improve student success -- yet many of these strategies remain under-implemented, siloed in pilot programs, or absent from institutional practice altogether. This page synthesizes findings from across the research corpus to identify high-impact opportunities that institutions are largely leaving on the table. Each opportunity is backed by empirical evidence or strong logical support and represents an area where broader adoption could meaningfully move the needle on student persistence, completion, and equity.

The common thread across these opportunities is a gap between what the evidence shows and what institutions actually do. In most cases, the barrier is not cost or feasibility but institutional inertia, fragmented decision-making, or lack of awareness.

## 1. Condensed Course Formats as an Equity Lever

Condensed courses -- those compressing a standard 16-week semester into 10 weeks or fewer -- are one of the most evidence-backed but under-scaled strategies available to community colleges.

**What the evidence shows:**

- Condensed formats increase pass rates (C or higher) by **+3.1 percentage points** and subsequent enrollment in the same field by **+3.2 pp**, based on a large-scale causal study of ~180,000 students across the Virginia Community College System (Li 2025).
- These gains are not explained by grading leniency: students who took condensed courses performed equivalently in subsequent courses when section-level controls were applied.
- The benefits are **disproportionately large for adult learners (25+) and underrepresented minority students**. Adult learners saw subsequent enrollment gains of +6.1 pp; URM students saw downstream pass-rate gains of +4.7 pp vs. +1.9 pp for non-URM peers.
- These populations also **naturally gravitate toward condensed formats** -- 28% of condensed enrollees were adult learners vs. 15% of traditional enrollees -- meaning the format reaches those who benefit most without requiring targeted recruitment.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Only about 22% of course sections in the studied system were offered in condensed format.
- Condensed courses are more often taught by part-time faculty and delivered online -- conditions that might create institutional reluctance to expand them despite strong evidence.
- Faculty and administrators may harbor concerns about instructional quality and student burnout that the evidence does not support at scale.
- Virginia's VCCS launched its Accelerate Opportunity Initiative in 2024, mandating expanded 8-week terms, but most community college systems have no comparable policy.

**Practical illustration:** For a college with 1,000 first-time students, switching one traditional course per student to a condensed format would produce approximately 40 additional course completions with a C or higher.

## 2. Low-Cost AI Tutoring for Under-Resourced Contexts

AI-powered tutoring has reached a level of effectiveness and cost-efficiency that makes it one of the most promising -- yet least deployed -- tools for closing learning gaps, particularly in contexts where human tutoring is scarce or unaffordable.

**What the evidence shows:**

- The Rori AI math tutor, delivered via WhatsApp on basic phones, produced learning gains equivalent to approximately **one additional year of schooling** at a cost of roughly **$5 per student per year** (Henkel 2024). This represents one of the most cost-effective educational interventions documented in the literature.
- In a controlled study at Harvard, AI tutoring outperformed active learning by approximately **2x** in a physics course (Kestin 2025).
- Google's LearnLM-Tutor, when used under supervised conditions, proved at least as effective as expert human tutors, with **zero instances of harmful content** in over 2,600 conversation transcripts (LearnLM 2025).

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Only **23% of institutions** report having an AI strategy of any kind (Simunich 2025), and most AI tutoring deployments remain confined to research pilots or single-course implementations.
- Institutional procurement and IT governance processes are not designed for rapidly evolving AI tools.
- Faculty concerns about academic integrity and over-reliance on AI create resistance, even when the application is tutoring rather than assessment.
- The most cost-effective tools (e.g., WhatsApp-based delivery) are designed for low-resource global contexts and have not been adapted for U.S. higher education at scale.

## 3. Addressing the "Advising Cliff" After First Year

Institutions invest heavily in first-year advising but allow structured support to drop off sharply once students pass initial milestones, creating a gap precisely when students face increasingly complex decisions about majors, transfers, and careers.

**What the evidence shows:**

- Over **90% of institutions** require advising before initial course registration, but this drops to approximately **50% after the first year** (Feygin 2022). The structured touchpoints that characterize first-year advising largely evaporate.
- Students with **multiple advising appointments** show **86.9% persistence** vs. **64.2% for those with none** -- a 22.7 percentage point gap. Even a single appointment is associated with 82.1% persistence (Goemans 2024).
- The association between advising frequency and positive outcomes holds across all demographic subgroups and remains significant when controlling for enrollment status, sex, and race.
- Only about **40% of institutions** proactively reach out to students who have accumulated enough credits to graduate but have not applied for graduation (Feygin 2022).
- Exemplary institutions in the Feygin study maintain structured advising touchpoints at milestones throughout the student journey (e.g., at 30 credits, 45 credits), while typical institutions do not.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Advising resources are finite, and institutions concentrate them where the perceived risk is highest (entry). This is rational but ignores mounting evidence that mid-program support matters.
- Advisor-to-student ratios average approximately 450:1 at typical institutions (Feygin 2022), making sustained engagement difficult without technology or workflow changes.
- The "advising cliff" is often invisible because institutions track first-year retention but not the quality of advising touchpoints in subsequent years.
- Career advising remains insufficiently integrated with academic advising at most institutions, even though students making mid-program decisions about majors and careers are the ones who need it most.

## 4. Course Scheduling as a Hidden Driver of Student Outcomes

Course scheduling is treated as a logistical exercise at most institutions, but research shows it functions as a structural determinant of student persistence -- one that is entangled with, and often mistaken for, instructor quality effects.

**What the evidence shows:**

- The widely cited negative effect of part-time faculty on student outcomes is **primarily a scheduling artifact**: adjunct instructors disproportionately teach at non-traditional times (evenings, weekends) that are independently associated with lower subsequent enrollment. When scheduling is controlled, the direct effect of part-time faculty status largely disappears (Ran 2020).
- This finding, based on 38,799 enrollments across six community colleges, reframes the part-time faculty problem as a scheduling problem -- a far more tractable challenge.
- Course scheduling optimization using computational methods can reduce sections needed by **14%** and save approximately **$130K annually** per institution (Xue 2024).
- Teacher assignment optimization improved preference satisfaction by roughly **30%** over manual assignments (Domenech 2016), suggesting that better algorithms benefit both students and faculty.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Most institutions still schedule courses manually using historical patterns, departmental preferences, and room availability -- not student demand data or outcome analysis.
- The Ran (2020) finding that scheduling, not instructor quality, drives the "adjunct effect" has not translated into widespread scheduling reform.
- Scheduling sits at the intersection of registrar, academic affairs, and facilities management, making it an orphaned function that no single office owns strategically.
- The tools for demand-driven scheduling optimization exist but require data infrastructure and cross-departmental coordination that few institutions have built.

## 5. Proactive Universal Design Instead of Reactive Accommodations

The dominant approach to disability and accessibility in higher education remains reactive -- accommodations are provided after students self-identify and request them -- despite strong evidence that proactive universal design benefits all learners and reduces institutional burden.

**What the evidence shows:**

- Approximately **19% of U.S. undergraduates** report a disability, and this proportion is increasing (Lomellini 2025). The actual figure is likely higher due to underreporting.
- Universal Design for Learning (UDL) benefits **all students**, not only those with documented disabilities. Flexible assessment, multimodal content delivery, and varied engagement options improve outcomes across the student population.
- Faculty largely **lack accessibility training** during graduate education, meaning they enter teaching without the knowledge to design inclusively from the start.
- The prevailing medical model of disability (treating it as an individual deficit requiring accommodation) has not fully given way to the social model (recognizing that environments create barriers), despite decades of advocacy and policy development.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Accommodation systems are legally mandated and well-established, giving institutions a compliance-oriented default that feels sufficient.
- UDL requires upfront investment in course design, faculty development, and instructional support -- costs that are harder to quantify than the marginal cost of individual accommodations.
- Disability services offices are typically under-resourced and focused on processing accommodation requests rather than driving institutional pedagogy.
- The increasing share of students with disabilities, combined with the expansion of recognized conditions (e.g., mental health, learning differences), will make the reactive model progressively less sustainable.

## 6. Leveraging Belonging Interventions at Scale

A growing body of research demonstrates that sense of belonging is a powerful and independent predictor of student persistence and completion -- yet belonging interventions remain largely peripheral to core academic operations.

**What the evidence shows:**

- A **1-point increase in belonging** (on a standard scale) is associated with a **3.4 percentage point increase in 4-year graduation rates** (Brady 2025). This effect is comparable in magnitude to thousands of dollars in additional financial aid.
- Brief, well-designed belonging interventions can shift student perceptions of whether they fit in college and improve persistence, particularly for students from underrepresented backgrounds (Murphy 2022, cited in Axim Briefing).
- Belonging effects operate through multiple channels: students who feel they belong are more likely to seek help, engage with coursework, persist through difficulty, and use institutional resources.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Belonging interventions are often designed and delivered as standalone programs (orientation activities, mentoring circles, first-year seminars) rather than embedded in the routine academic experience.
- Scaling belonging interventions requires faculty buy-in and pedagogical change, not just student affairs programming.
- Institutions may view belonging as a "soft" outcome that is less actionable than, say, financial aid or advising -- despite evidence that its effect size is comparable.
- Measurement of belonging is inconsistent across institutions, making it difficult to benchmark or track systematically.
- The students who would benefit most from belonging interventions (first-generation, low-income, underrepresented minorities) are often the least likely to participate in optional programs where these interventions are housed.

## 7. Employer Co-Design of Credentials for Market Signal Clarity

Higher education credentials face a growing market signal problem: employers want skills-based hiring but struggle to interpret academic credentials, while institutions produce credentials without systematic input from the employers who will evaluate them.

**What the evidence shows:**

- **96% of employers** say micro-credentials strengthen job applications (Coursera 2025), yet employer recognition of specific credentials remains inconsistent and limited (Tamoliune 2023, Varadarajan 2023).
- **85% of employers** say durable skills are best developed through experience rather than coursework (UpSkill America 2024), suggesting a disconnect between what credentials certify and what employers value.
- Higher education-employer partnerships are **declining**: four-year institution partnerships dropped from **49% to 40%** and community college partnerships from **45% to 38%** between 2022 and 2023 (Etter 2024).
- Meanwhile, private providers are filling the gap: LinkedIn Learning partnerships grew from **44% to 52%** in the same period, and professional association partnerships surged from 29% to 46% (Etter 2024).
- The top barriers employers cite for working with higher education are cost (37%), lack of real-world application (25%), and slow turnaround (22%) (Etter 2024). These are addressable through co-design rather than fundamental structural change.
- Despite declining partnerships, **98% of employers** currently partnering with four-year institutions plan to continue -- indicating high satisfaction once partnerships are established. The problem is initiation, not retention.

**Why it remains overlooked:**

- Curriculum design in higher education is faculty-driven and committee-governed, with employer input typically limited to advisory boards that meet once or twice per year.
- Institutions view employer co-design as a threat to academic autonomy rather than a complement to it.
- The fragmented credential landscape (degrees, certificates, badges, micro-credentials) makes it difficult for employers to provide coherent input on what they would recognize.
- Community colleges, which have historically been the strongest bridge between education and employment, are losing employer partnerships faster than four-year institutions.

## Implications for Action

These seven opportunities share several characteristics that institutions and policymakers should recognize:

**Most are structural, not pedagogical.** Condensed scheduling, course timing, advising continuity, and credential co-design are operational decisions that do not require faculty to change how they teach -- only how institutions organize learning.

**Many are equity multipliers.** Condensed formats, AI tutoring, sustained advising, universal design, and belonging interventions all show disproportionate benefits for the students who face the greatest structural barriers. Implementing them is not just an efficiency play but an equity strategy.

**The evidence base is sufficient for action.** While additional research is always valuable, the strategies identified here are not speculative. They are supported by causal or quasi-experimental evidence, large-scale observational studies, or convergent findings across multiple contexts.

**The barriers are institutional, not financial.** With the possible exception of AI tutoring infrastructure, none of these strategies require large capital investments. They require coordination, policy change, and willingness to reorganize existing resources.

Institutions that move first on these opportunities stand to gain meaningful advantages in student outcomes, equity metrics, and employer relevance -- areas where competitive pressure is intensifying and public accountability is growing.
