## Overview

Academic readiness -- the preparation students bring to college-level coursework -- and the design of courses that meet students where they are represent two of the most consequential levers for improving postsecondary outcomes. Up to 65% of college students take at least one remedial course, yet traditional developmental education sequences have been shown to delay degree progress, discourage persistence, and disproportionately affect low-income students and students of color. Over the past decade, a wave of reforms -- corequisite models, multiple measures placement, math pathways, condensed course formats, and early postsecondary options -- has reshaped how institutions approach readiness. More than half of U.S. states now mandate or recommend developmental education reforms, though implementation with fidelity still reaches fewer than 50% of college students (Axim Strategy Briefing).

Simultaneously, course design innovations -- inclusive pedagogy, technology-enabled supports, competency-based assessment, and AI-powered tutoring -- are expanding what is possible inside the classroom. The COVID-19 pandemic intensified urgency around these reforms: learning losses wiped out roughly two decades of growth in student achievement, with disproportionate impacts on low-income learners (Axim Strategy Briefing). Rebuilding academic readiness at scale requires both structural reforms to placement and developmental education and pedagogical innovations that personalize the learning experience.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

**Developmental education (remediation):** Courses designed to build foundational skills in math, English, or reading for students assessed as not ready for college-level work. Traditionally structured as prerequisite sequences that must be completed before students can enroll in credit-bearing courses.

**Corequisite model:** A reform approach in which students enroll directly in college-level gateway courses while simultaneously receiving additional academic support (e.g., a paired lab, tutoring session, or supplemental instruction section) rather than completing prerequisite remedial courses first.

**Multiple measures placement:** The use of at least two sources of evidence -- such as a standardized test score combined with high school GPA, non-cognitive assessments, or prior coursework -- to determine course placement, rather than relying on a single standardized test.

**Gateway courses:** The foundational, typically introductory college-level courses in math and English that serve as prerequisites for degree progress. High DFW (D grade, failure, or withdrawal) rates in these courses are a primary bottleneck for completion, particularly among low-income and first-generation students.

**Math pathways:** Structured sequences that align college-level math requirements with students' programs of study (e.g., statistics for social science majors, quantitative reasoning for humanities majors) rather than requiring all students to take college algebra.

**Condensed course formats:** Courses that compress the same credit hours and content of a traditional 16-week semester into a shorter timeframe, most commonly 8 or 10 weeks (Li & Xu, 2025).

**Adaptive learning technology:** Digital platforms that adjust instructional content, pacing, and feedback in real time based on individual learner performance and diagnosed knowledge gaps.

**AI-powered tutoring:** The use of large language models or other AI systems to deliver personalized, one-on-one instructional support -- including Socratic questioning, scaffolded hints, and targeted feedback -- at scale.

**Competency-based assessment:** Assessment approaches that evaluate students on demonstrated mastery of defined skills and knowledge rather than time spent in a course, emphasizing authentic, real-world tasks over traditional exams (Cano et al., 2023).

**Constructive alignment:** A pedagogical framework (Biggs & Tang) ensuring coherence among intended learning outcomes, teaching and learning activities, and assessment tasks (Salinas-Navarro et al., 2024).

## Current State of Research

### Developmental Education Reform

The evidence base for developmental education reform has matured significantly, though innovation has slowed and scaling with fidelity remains a challenge.

**Corequisite models** represent the most broadly adopted reform. Students placed into corequisite support were 13 percentage points more likely to pass gateway math and 15 percentage points more likely to pass gateway English than peers taking traditional prerequisite remedial courses (Axim Strategy Briefing). These gains are substantial given the large population affected, but results depend heavily on implementation quality, instructor training, and the availability of wraparound supports.

**Multiple measures placement** has emerged as the dominant alternative to single-test placement. A large-scale randomized study by CAPR, covering 12 community colleges and nearly 30,000 students across Minnesota, New York, and Wisconsin, found that students who were "bumped up" into college-level courses through multiple measures assessment (using high school GPA alongside test scores) were eight percentage points more likely to pass a college-level course and earned 2.0 more credits on average than comparable peers in the test-only group. These students were also 1.5 percentage points more likely to earn a degree or transfer to a four-year institution within nine semesters (CAPR, n.d.). About three-quarters of colleges now use multiple measures assessment systems, and the cost of implementation is modest -- roughly $60 per student -- while reducing students' developmental course-taking and associated tuition costs.

**Math pathways** have shown strong results: students in aligned pathways were 11 percentage points more likely to pass a college-level math course during their first year, with the largest gains among part-time students and those recommended for multi-course developmental sequences (Axim Strategy Briefing). These reforms address a long-standing critique that requiring college algebra of all students, regardless of major, creates unnecessary barriers.

**Early postsecondary options** -- including AP, IB, dual enrollment, and Early College High Schools (ECHS) -- offer another strategy for building readiness before college entry. Giani et al. (2023) studied the University of Texas at Austin's OnRamps dual enrollment program, which by 2022-23 enrolled over 41,500 students across nearly 200 school districts. Using inverse probability weighting with regression adjustment (IPWRA) across more than 2 million Texas high school graduates, the researchers found that earning OnRamps credit was associated with a 10.3 percentage-point increase in four-year bachelor's degree attainment. The benefits were largest for underrepresented students: Black students who earned OnRamps credit saw an 88.7% increase in their likelihood of earning a bachelor's degree, compared to 41.1% for White students. Each additional OnRamps course a student completed further increased their probability of degree attainment, with estimates of 6.2 percentage points for one course, 7.9 for two, and 15.9 for three courses. The Axim Strategy Briefing notes that comprehensive models like ECHS with a career focus show the strongest evidence, while lighter-touch early postsecondary models produce more mixed results.

**Summer bridge programs**, which combine academic acceleration with broader coaching for the high school-to-college transition, remain a complementary strategy, though rigorous evidence on their long-term effects is still emerging (Axim Strategy Briefing).

### Course Redesign and Pedagogy

Beyond placement and developmental education, the design of courses themselves -- format, modality, assessment, and pedagogy -- has a significant bearing on student success.

**Condensed course formats** have gained traction, particularly at community colleges. Li and Xu (2025) provide the first large-scale causal evidence on their effects, drawing on data from approximately 180,000 first-time students in the Virginia Community College System (2010-2015). Using a two-way fixed effects model with student and college-by-course fixed effects, they found that condensed courses (10 weeks or fewer) increased pass rates (with a grade of C or higher) by 3.1 percentage points relative to traditional 16-week courses. Students in condensed formats were also 3.2 percentage points more likely to enroll in a subsequent course in the same field, and subsequent course performance was maintained -- indicating the gains were not attributable to reduced rigor. Critically, the benefits were largest for populations facing the greatest structural barriers: adult learners (25+) saw a 6.1 percentage-point increase in subsequent course enrollment, and underrepresented minority students saw a 4.7 percentage-point increase in downstream course completion. Condensed courses are disproportionately enrolled in by adult learners (28% vs. 15% in traditional formats) and Black students (26% vs. 22%), suggesting that expanding these formats could advance equity goals.

**Online and blended learning** has produced mixed evidence. Akpen et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of 18 studies on online learning during and after the pandemic, finding that the majority reported neutral or positive effects on academic performance -- students benefited from flexibility, recorded lectures, and self-paced review. However, student engagement consistently suffered: learners reported social isolation, reduced peer and instructor interaction, and difficulty with hands-on or clinical coursework. The contrast between adequate performance and poor engagement suggests that online modalities require deliberate design for interaction and community-building to be effective.

**Competency-based and authentic assessment** remains aspirational at many institutions. Cano et al. (2023) interviewed 16 instructors and 15 students at the University of Barcelona during COVID-era emergency remote teaching and found that generic competencies (teamwork, communication, self-regulated learning) were largely absent from assessment practices. Only 37.5% of instructors reported both teaching and assessing generic competencies, and two-thirds of students perceived assessment as purely summative. Digital tools were used primarily for content delivery rather than formative learning. The pandemic did not catalyze a shift toward competency-based assessment; instead, instructors largely replicated face-to-face practices online, focusing on e-proctoring rather than authentic evaluation.

**Generative AI for course design** is an emerging area. Salinas-Navarro et al. (2024) explored how GenAI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Bard, Copilot) can support experiential learning and authentic assessment through constructive alignment. Using a thing-ethnography methodology, they identified roles for GenAI beyond writing assistance: as agents for formulating learning outcomes, creating realistic case studies and simulations, facilitating reflective practice, and enabling adaptive assessments. Their framework positions GenAI tools as supports across all stages of Kolb's experiential learning cycle -- generating concrete experiences, prompting reflective observation, assisting abstract conceptualization, and enabling active experimentation. While the findings are conceptual rather than empirical, they outline a practical blueprint for integrating GenAI into pedagogical design.

### AI-Powered Tutoring and Adaptive Learning

AI-powered tutoring has produced some of the most striking recent evidence in educational technology, with multiple rigorous studies showing substantial learning gains.

**Kestin et al. (2025)** conducted a randomized crossover study with 194 Harvard undergraduates in introductory physics, comparing learning from an AI tutor (built on GPT-4 with pedagogical guardrails) against a high-quality active learning classroom. Students in the AI tutor condition demonstrated learning gains more than double those of the active lecture group (standardized effect of 0.63 to 1.3 standard deviations, p < 10^-8), completed the material in less time (median 49 minutes vs. 60), and reported significantly higher engagement and motivation. The AI tutor's design incorporated seven pedagogical principles: active engagement (not giving away answers), cognitive load management (brief responses), growth mindset support, scaffolding, accuracy enrichment (step-by-step solutions in prompts), timely personalized feedback, and self-pacing. One notable finding: prior experience with ChatGPT correlated negatively with outcomes, suggesting that unguided AI use undermines structured pedagogical design.

**The LearnLM team (Google & Eedi, 2025)** ran an exploratory RCT with 165 students across five UK secondary schools. LearnLM, a model fine-tuned for Socratic pedagogy and supervised by expert human tutors, matched or exceeded human tutors on all learning outcomes. Students tutored by LearnLM were 5.5 percentage points more likely to solve novel problems on subsequent topics than those tutored by humans alone (66.2% vs. 60.7%), with a posterior probability of 93.6% that LearnLM outperformed. The model generated 3,617 messages with zero instances of harmful content and only 5 factual errors (0.1%). Tutors approved 74.4% of AI-drafted messages without edits. An unexpected finding was that three of five tutors reported learning new pedagogical techniques from supervising the AI, suggesting a professional development benefit.

**Henkel et al. (2024)** evaluated Rori, a WhatsApp-based AI math tutor deployed across 11 schools in Ghana. In this RCT with approximately 500 students in grades 3-9, treatment students who received one hour per week of Rori access showed learning gains with an effect size of Cohen's d = 0.36 (p < 0.001), roughly equivalent to an additional year of learning -- at a cost of approximately $5 per student per year. Rori operates on basic mobile phones with 3G connectivity, addressing the infrastructure constraints that have limited prior technology interventions in low- and middle-income countries.

**Zhang et al. (2025)** conducted a meta-analysis of 13 empirical studies across eight countries examining AI technologies in education and found an overall effect size of Hedges' g = 0.86 (95% CI [0.45, 1.27], p < 0.0001), qualifying as a large effect. Chatbots and generative AI showed the strongest subgroup effect (g = 1.02), while learning management systems and AI platforms showed more modest effects (g = 0.62). Substantial heterogeneity across studies (I-squared = 86.15%) underscores that implementation context matters significantly.

Collectively, these studies suggest that AI tutoring can approach or replicate Bloom's 2-sigma effect of one-on-one human tutoring -- personalized feedback, adaptive pacing, and reduced cognitive load -- at dramatically lower cost and greater scale. However, several caveats apply: most studies are short-term, conducted in controlled settings, and focused on well-structured domains (math and physics). The role of human oversight, the risk of student over-reliance, and generalizability to less structured disciplines remain open questions.

## Challenges and Barriers

**Scaling reforms with fidelity.** Despite strong evidence for corequisites, math pathways, and multiple measures placement, these reforms still reach fewer than 50% of college students. Innovation in developmental education has slowed, and institutional inertia, faculty resistance, and uneven state policy environments impede broader adoption (Axim Strategy Briefing).

**COVID-19 learning loss.** The pandemic wiped out roughly two decades of growth in student achievement, disproportionately affecting low-income students (Axim Strategy Briefing). Rebuilding foundational skills at the postsecondary level requires new approaches to acceleration, and institutions cannot simply wait for the K-12 system to catch up.

**Equity gaps in gateway courses.** Low-income and first-generation students continue to experience disproportionately high DFW rates in gateway courses, even as placement reforms have expanded access to college-level coursework (Axim Strategy Briefing). Access without adequate support can lead to failure at the gateway level.

**Assessment literacy.** Both instructors and students lack fluency in competency-based and formative assessment practices. Cano et al. (2023) found that most instructors defaulted to summative assessment during the pandemic, and students perceived evaluation as focused on proving content knowledge rather than supporting learning. Without shifts in assessment culture, pedagogical innovations remain surface-level.

**Technology access and the digital divide.** Online and technology-enabled learning depends on reliable internet, adequate devices, and digital literacy -- conditions not uniformly met, particularly for students in rural areas, low-income households, and low- and middle-income countries (Akpen et al., 2024; Henkel et al., 2024). While mobile-first solutions like Rori show promise in addressing infrastructure constraints, the digital divide remains a barrier to equitable implementation.

**Student engagement in online environments.** Across multiple studies, online learning consistently reduced student engagement, peer interaction, and sense of belonging, even when academic performance held steady (Akpen et al., 2024). Science and health professions students reported particular difficulty with the absence of hands-on and clinical experiences.

**AI implementation risks.** Unguided student use of AI can undermine learning -- Kestin et al. (2025) found that prior ChatGPT experience negatively correlated with learning gains from a structured AI tutor. The LearnLM trial (2025) demonstrated that human oversight remains essential: tutors intervened in 25.6% of AI-generated messages, primarily to manage pacing and social-emotional nuance. Questions of academic integrity, over-reliance, and the applicability of AI tutoring beyond well-structured STEM domains remain unresolved.

## Best Practices and Recommendations

**Placement and developmental education:**
- Adopt multiple measures placement using high school GPA alongside test scores; CAPR evidence shows this is a low-cost ($60 per student) intervention that increases college-level course completion and credit accumulation.
- Implement corequisite models as the default rather than prerequisite remedial sequences, ensuring paired support is adequately resourced with trained instructors and tutoring.
- Align math requirements with programs of study through math pathways, prioritizing implementation for part-time students and those recommended for multi-course sequences, who show the largest gains.

**Course format and modality:**
- Expand condensed course formats (8- and 10-week terms), particularly at community colleges serving adult learners and underrepresented minority students, who benefit disproportionately from these formats (Li & Xu, 2025).
- Design online and blended courses with intentional structures for interaction: discussion forums, group projects, instructor-student check-ins, and multimedia resources. Flexibility alone is insufficient without engagement scaffolding (Akpen et al., 2024).

**Assessment and pedagogy:**
- Shift from summative-only assessment to integrated formative and authentic assessment practices. Invest in faculty development around assessment literacy, competency-based design, and the use of learning analytics for formative purposes (Cano et al., 2023).
- Use GenAI tools to support course design -- formulating learning outcomes, generating realistic case studies, creating adaptive quizzes, and facilitating peer review -- while maintaining human judgment in pedagogical decisions (Salinas-Navarro et al., 2024).

**AI-powered tutoring:**
- Design AI tutors with explicit pedagogical guardrails: scaffolded hints rather than direct answers, brief responses to manage cognitive load, growth mindset framing, and accuracy enrichment through curated solution prompts (Kestin et al., 2025).
- Maintain human oversight in deployment, particularly for social-emotional calibration and pacing decisions that AI currently handles poorly (LearnLM, 2025).
- Explore mobile-first, low-cost AI tutoring solutions for resource-constrained contexts, following the Rori model of WhatsApp-based delivery at $5 per student per year (Henkel et al., 2024).
- Train students on effective AI use: structured engagement with AI tutors produces learning gains; unstructured use can undermine them.

**Early postsecondary options:**
- Prioritize comprehensive dual enrollment models with university-level rigor, professional development for high school instructors, and separate grading (as in OnRamps) over lighter-touch models. Focus outreach on underrepresented students, who show the largest benefits (Giani et al., 2023).
- Create pathways for students to complete three to four dual enrollment courses during high school, given the cumulative dosage effects on bachelor's degree attainment.

## Implications for Practice

The convergence of developmental education reform, course design innovation, and AI-enabled personalization creates an opportunity to fundamentally rethink how institutions support academic readiness. Several practical implications stand out:

1. **Readiness is not a fixed trait but a design problem.** The evidence on corequisites, multiple measures placement, and condensed formats shows that many students classified as "not ready" succeed when given direct access to college-level courses with appropriate support. Institutions should treat placement as an access decision, not a gatekeeping function.

2. **Format flexibility advances equity.** Condensed courses, online options, and early postsecondary programs disproportionately serve and benefit students who face the greatest structural barriers -- adult learners, working students, underrepresented minorities, and first-generation college-goers. Expanding these options is an equity strategy, not just a scheduling convenience.

3. **AI tutoring can address Bloom's 2-sigma problem at scale.** Multiple rigorous studies now show that well-designed AI tutors can produce learning gains comparable to one-on-one human tutoring. The key is pedagogical design, not raw AI capability. Institutions should invest in AI tutoring infrastructure while building faculty capacity to supervise and complement these tools.

4. **Assessment reform is the lagging indicator.** Placement and course format reforms have advanced faster than assessment practices, which remain predominantly summative and content-focused. Without parallel investment in formative, authentic, and competency-based assessment, the potential of pedagogical innovation will remain unrealized.

5. **Post-pandemic urgency persists.** The learning losses from COVID-19 are not self-correcting. Postsecondary institutions will continue to enroll students with weakened foundational skills for years to come, requiring sustained investment in accelerated, supportive pathways.
