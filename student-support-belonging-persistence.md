## Overview

Student success in postsecondary education depends on far more than academic preparation. A growing body of research demonstrates that comprehensive support systems -- encompassing academic advising, a sense of belonging, wraparound services, and quality instruction -- are critical to student persistence and credential attainment. Postsecondary institutions can assume that all students need some level of academic support beyond classes, and the evidence increasingly points toward coordinated, institution-wide approaches that accompany students through the full learning journey.

The trend toward comprehensive student supports reflects a recognition that academic, social, and logistical barriers are interconnected. Programs like CUNY's Accelerated Study in Associate Programs (ASAP), which combine advising, financial assistance, transportation support, and community-building, have demonstrated that coordinated supports measurably increase persistence and graduation rates. The challenge for institutions is moving from siloed interventions to integrated systems that address students holistically -- as learners, as people navigating complex lives, and as future professionals.

This page synthesizes research on the major dimensions of student support: advising structures, belonging and identity, online learning accessibility, and the role of faculty and instructional quality in student outcomes.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Academic advising:** A structured educational practice that helps students clarify and attain academic, career, and personal goals through sustained relationships with trained professionals or faculty. Effective advising is sustained, strategic, integrated, proactive, and personalized.
- **Sense of belonging:** A student's perception of being included in and accepted by their educational environment. Rooted in Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs and theorized as a fundamental psychological need (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), belonging influences help-seeking, engagement, and persistence.
- **Wraparound supports:** Non-academic services addressing barriers such as housing insecurity, transportation, food access, childcare, and financial emergencies that disproportionately affect first-generation, low-income, and racially minoritized students.
- **Learning communities:** Structured programs that place students into small sub-groups, often co-enrolling them in linked courses, to foster peer relationships and faculty connections.
- **First-generation students:** Students for whom neither parent holds a bachelor's degree. These students face distinctive navigational, financial, and cultural challenges in higher education.
- **Universal Design for Learning (UDL):** A framework developed by CAST that provides multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression to create flexible, inclusive learning experiences.
- **Persistence:** Continued enrollment toward credential completion, measured term-to-term or year-to-year. Distinct from retention (an institutional measure), persistence reflects the student's trajectory.
- **Student-centered campus:** An institutional design approach that uses backward design and process-mapping from the student's perspective to reduce bureaucratic friction and improve support delivery.

## Current State of Research

### Academic Advising

Academic advising has emerged as one of the most consequential interventions for student success, particularly at community colleges and open-access institutions where students face multiple barriers to completion. Research consistently demonstrates positive associations between advising contact and student outcomes including persistence, credit accumulation, and degree attainment.

**Frequency and intensity of advising matter.** Goemans and Kapinos (2024) studied over 34,000 students at a large multicampus community college system and found a robust statistical association between advising appointment frequency and four success metrics. Students with no advising appointments persisted at a rate of 64.2%, compared to 82.1% for students with one appointment and 86.9% for those with multiple appointments. This association held when controlling for sex, enrollment status, and race. Students with more frequent advising also registered for subsequent semesters 6-9 days earlier and completed 1.3-1.6 more credits per term than those with no advising appointments.

**Advising structures vary widely across institutions.** Feygin et al. (2022) surveyed 47 colleges and found that nearly all institutions (90%+) require advising before initial course registration, and approximately 80% assign students a specific advisor during the first term. However, required advising drops sharply after the first year -- from roughly 90% to 50% -- creating what the researchers call an "advising cliff." Average advisor-to-student ratios are approximately 450:1 at typical institutions but closer to 300:1 at institutions recognized for strong advising. Approximately 65% of institutions use hybrid staffing models combining professional advisors and faculty advisors. The trend is toward professional advising models, with colleges increasingly moving from faculty advisors to dedicated professional advisors.

**Technology integration remains uneven.** Feygin et al. (2022) found that while degree audit software (75%) and early alert systems (70%) are common, more advanced tools like predictive analytics (25%) and AI-assisted advising (10%) remain rare. Chamberlain and Parnell (2022) note that technology solutions can help advisors identify and address risks early, but integration is a persistent pain point. Institutions serving high proportions of Pell-eligible students report lower student engagement with advising technologies, potentially reflecting inequities in off-campus access to devices and internet connectivity.

**Equity-centered advising requires intentional design.** Chamberlain and Parnell (2022) argue that achieving equitable student outcomes at scale requires institution-wide collaboration, data-informed decision-making that centers student perspectives, investment in supportive technologies, and ongoing professional development. They highlight "culturally engaging advising" as a practice that cultivates meaningful relationships, connects students with relevant resources, understands the interconnected nature of students' lives, and links students with learning opportunities relevant to their communities and identities. Approximately 60% of institutions report having advising practices specifically designed for underserved populations, and about 50% have specific protocols for first-generation students (Feygin et al., 2022).

### Belonging and Persistence

Research on belonging has moved from a peripheral concern to a central focus of student success strategy. The evidence base linking belonging to persistence and credential attainment has grown substantially, with nationally representative longitudinal data now demonstrating associations with graduation outcomes.

**Belonging predicts credential attainment.** Brady and Gopalan (2025), using nationally representative data from over 21,700 undergraduates who began college in 2011-2012, found that a one-point increase on a five-point belonging scale corresponded to a 3.4 percentage-point increase in four-year credential attainment and a 2.7 percentage-point increase in six-year attainment. These findings held across institution types. Moreover, among students at four-year colleges, change in belonging over time independently predicted attainment, suggesting that what happens after the first year continues to shape outcomes.

**The belonging-attainment relationship is broadly consistent but not uniform.** Brady and Gopalan (2025) found two significant moderating effects: the association between belonging and four-year graduation was stronger for continuing-generation students than for first-generation peers, and the belonging-attainment link was weaker for Asian students at six years compared to non-Asian students. The researchers caution that first-generation students may face structural and psychological challenges -- limited guidance navigating college systems, financial pressures, cultural mismatches -- that can modulate the protective effects of belonging (Bay, 2025).

**Belonging interventions can be low-cost and effective.** Brief interventions that normalize academic and social challenges have been shown to shift students' perceptions of their experience and lead to higher persistence. Brady notes that structuring first-year seminars and addressing procedural hurdles such as class registration can meaningfully improve belonging with relatively few resources (Bay, 2025). The impact of belonging on persistence and graduation has been compared to the effect of thousands of dollars in additional financial aid.

**Integration of belonging into academics is a growing priority.** Historically, many belonging practices have been separate from the core academic experience -- housed in student affairs, orientation programs, or extracurricular activities. Recent efforts aim to integrate belonging into academic structures through learning communities, first-year experience courses, and inclusive pedagogy. Learning communities that place students into small sub-groups help develop relationships with peers and faculty, creating academic belonging that connects directly to course engagement and success.

**First-generation students face compounding challenges.** The Pell Institute (Simone & Christopher, 2025) reports a dramatic decline in bachelor's degree expectations among high school students: from 71.6% in 2002 to 43.6% in 2022. Among first-generation students specifically, the decline was from 59.9% to 32.6%. Uncertainty about educational attainment has surged, with 31.1% of first-generation high school students in 2022 unsure about their highest expected degree, compared to 13.9% in 2002. These trends in declining expectations make belonging and support even more critical for converting enrollment into completion. At community colleges, 74% of students are first-generation (Brady & Gopalan, 2025), and these students face barriers including less familiarity navigating higher education bureaucracy, financial burdens, housing insecurity, transportation challenges, and the demands of balancing work and family.

**Wraparound supports are essential for populations facing structural barriers.** Community colleges serve a disproportionate number of first-generation, low-income, and racially minoritized students. Comprehensive models like CUNY ASAP work to remove everyday barriers such as transportation costs, complicated scheduling, and limited advising, and have been shown to improve graduation rates while helping students feel connected to campus (Bay, 2025). The evidence supports a shift toward student support models that are comprehensive and accompany students through the full learning journey.

### Online Learning and Access

The rapid expansion of online learning, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has introduced both opportunities for access and significant challenges for student engagement and equity.

**Online learning's impact on performance is mixed, but engagement consistently declines.** Akpen et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of 18 studies and found that nine reported positive impacts on student performance from online learning, while three reported declines. However, the picture for engagement was markedly different: students consistently reported reduced interaction with peers and instructors, feelings of isolation, and decreased ability to participate in collaborative or practical learning activities. Students who reported being able to engage actively with peers and instructors attributed their success to self-motivation.

**The digital divide remains a significant barrier.** The CHLOE 10 survey (Simunich et al., 2025) found that 88% of chief online learning officers said the digital divide affects at least some of their online students. At public two-year institutions, 38% reported the divide affects many students, and 0% of community college respondents said the divide was nonexistent. Singh et al. (2024) identified technology access and internet connectivity as persistent challenges, particularly in developing regions and among economically disadvantaged students. Montenegro et al. (2024) confirmed that socioeconomic disparities become more visible in online environments, with rural students facing particular challenges.

**Accessibility for students with disabilities requires systemic attention.** Lomellini et al. (2025), reviewing 91 sources on accessible online learning, found that approximately 19% of U.S. undergraduates report having a disability. Common barriers in online courses include inaccessible documents, uncaptioned videos, timed assessments, and inflexible assignment formats. The field is gradually shifting from a medical model of disability (reactive accommodations) to a social model (proactive universal design), but implementation remains inconsistent. Most faculty lack training in Universal Design for Learning, and accessibility is often treated as optional rather than essential. The researchers argue that UDL implementation benefits all students, not just those with disabilities.

**Institutional support for online learners is improving but uneven.** Simunich et al. (2025) found improvements in online learner support from CHLOE 6 to CHLOE 10: accessibility services rated as "in place and working well" rose from 32% to 52%, and academic advising and library services scored highest at 75-80%. However, student clubs and organizations for online learners declined (from 20% to 15%), and mental health and work placement services remained at the bottom (25-30%). Only 28% of institutions reported having documented academic continuity plans.

**Faculty readiness for online teaching has stagnated.** Despite the pandemic experience, 77% of faculty were not fully prepared for online teaching in 2025, virtually unchanged from 75% in 2020 (Simunich et al., 2025). Only 28% of institutions rated their faculty as fully prepared for online course design. Faculty training has tended to focus on tools rather than online pedagogy, and faculty consistently express a desire for more pedagogical -- not just technical -- professional development (Montenegro et al., 2024).

### Faculty and Instructional Quality

The composition and preparation of the instructional workforce has significant implications for student outcomes, particularly at community colleges where reliance on part-time faculty is heaviest.

**Adjunct faculty dominate online teaching.** Simunich et al. (2025) found that nearly half (47%) of chief online learning officers reported adjuncts in the "large + very large" category of online teaching distribution. There is a notable preference gap between faculty and students regarding modality: 55% of instructors prefer fully face-to-face teaching compared to just 31% of students.

**Part-time faculty effects are driven by structural factors, not individual quality.** Ran and Sanders (2020), studying 38,799 course enrollments at six community colleges, found that part-time faculty were associated with negative effects on subsequent course enrollment. However, when controlling for course scheduling characteristics, the negative effect substantially diminished or disappeared. Part-time faculty disproportionately teach at non-traditional times (evenings, weekends) associated with lower subsequent enrollment. The researchers conclude that the "part-time faculty effect" is largely a scheduling effect, and that simply replacing adjuncts with full-time faculty may not address the underlying problem.

**Adjunct effects vary by discipline.** Bettinger and Long (forthcoming), using data from public four-year colleges in Ohio, found that adjunct and graduate assistant instructors generally reduce subsequent interest in a subject relative to full-time faculty, but the effects are small and discipline-dependent. In humanities, adjuncts and graduate assistants had negative effects, while in some technical and professional fields, they had positive effects -- potentially because practitioners bring relevant industry experience.

**Faculty-student interaction matters for performance.** Multiple studies confirm that instructor-student interaction positively affects student performance in both online and face-to-face settings. Akpen et al. (2024) found that students who reported strong engagement with instructors performed better, and that instructor motivation and support were key drivers of engagement. Montenegro et al. (2024) noted that faculty see their workload increase substantially in online teaching, with preparing materials, responding to communications, and providing feedback all requiring more time than face-to-face equivalents.

## Challenges and Barriers

1. **The advising cliff after the first year.** Required advising touchpoints drop dramatically after the first year, even as students face new challenges around major selection, transfer, and professional preparation (Feygin et al., 2022).

2. **Fragmented support systems.** Academic advising, career services, financial aid, and personal supports often operate in silos, with limited coordination and data sharing across offices (Chamberlain & Parnell, 2022).

3. **Structural inequities in online learning.** The digital divide -- in device access, internet connectivity, and digital literacy -- disproportionately affects low-income, rural, and first-generation students. Public two-year institutions face the greatest impact (Simunich et al., 2025).

4. **Faculty preparedness stagnation.** Despite pandemic-driven adoption, faculty readiness for online course design and teaching has not meaningfully improved since 2020, with three-quarters of faculty still not fully prepared (Simunich et al., 2025).

5. **Belonging as a one-size-fits-all approach.** The relationship between belonging and outcomes varies across student populations. First-generation students, Asian students, and other subgroups may experience belonging differently due to structural, cultural, and financial factors (Brady & Gopalan, 2025).

6. **Declining educational expectations.** The steep drop in bachelor's degree expectations -- from 72.7% in 2009 to 43.6% in 2022 -- and rising uncertainty among high school students, especially first-generation students, pose upstream challenges for postsecondary enrollment and completion (Simone & Christopher, 2025).

7. **Accessibility as afterthought.** Despite legal requirements, most online courses remain inaccessible to students with disabilities. Reactive accommodation models place the burden on students to disclose and self-advocate, and faculty training in UDL remains insufficient (Lomellini et al., 2025).

8. **Part-time faculty working conditions.** Community colleges rely heavily on adjunct instructors who often lack institutional support, office space, professional development, and integration into departmental cultures. Scheduling practices that assign adjuncts to non-traditional time slots compound negative effects on student outcomes (Ran & Sanders, 2020).

## Best Practices and Recommendations

### Academic Advising
- Extend structured advising touchpoints beyond the first year, maintaining required interactions at key milestones (30 credits, 45 credits, program entry, nearing completion).
- Adopt case management advising models with manageable caseloads (300:1 or lower) and professional advisors trained in developmental and culturally engaging approaches.
- Integrate career advising with academic advising to connect educational planning with career preparation.
- Invest in advising technology (CRM platforms, early alert systems, degree audit tools) through a collaborative selection process that involves both advisors and students.
- Implement proactive completion interventions to identify and reach students with sufficient credits who have not applied for graduation.

### Belonging and Student Identity
- Measure belonging systematically using validated instruments, and adopt common measures to enable cross-institution comparison.
- Integrate belonging-building practices into academic structures (first-year seminars, learning communities, inclusive pedagogy) rather than treating them as separate from the core experience.
- Design brief, targeted interventions that normalize academic and social challenges, particularly during the first year.
- Apply backward design from the student perspective to reduce bureaucratic barriers (registration, financial aid, scheduling) that erode belonging.
- Recognize that belonging strategies must be tailored to institutional context and student population, rather than applied uniformly.

### Online Learning and Accessibility
- Adopt Universal Design for Learning as a foundational design principle rather than an accommodation add-on. Build accessibility into course templates, design processes, and procurement decisions.
- Address the digital divide through loaner equipment programs, on-campus computing resources, digital literacy workshops, and OER adoption.
- Provide sustained pedagogical (not just technical) professional development for faculty teaching online, with recognition of the additional workload online teaching requires.
- Ensure interactive elements -- discussion forums, multimedia resources, group projects -- are embedded in online courses to mitigate isolation and disengagement.
- Include accessibility evaluation criteria in LMS and third-party tool procurement processes.

### Faculty and Instruction
- Review scheduling practices for part-time faculty to avoid concentrating adjunct-taught sections at times associated with lower persistence.
- Provide part-time faculty with institutional support, professional development, and integration into departmental culture comparable to their teaching contribution.
- Align support services (advising, tutoring, library access) with the full range of class times, including evenings and weekends when adjunct sections are concentrated.
- Invest in faculty development that addresses both online pedagogy and inclusive teaching practices.

### Wraparound Supports
- Adopt comprehensive support models (such as CUNY ASAP) that address financial, logistical, and academic barriers simultaneously.
- Use data to identify students at risk and connect them proactively with relevant services.
- Map institutional processes from the student perspective to identify and reduce friction points in registration, financial aid, advising, and degree completion.

## Implications for Practice

The convergence of evidence across advising, belonging, online learning, and faculty quality research points toward several strategic imperatives for postsecondary institutions:

**Move from isolated interventions to coordinated support ecosystems.** The strongest outcomes emerge when advising, belonging initiatives, wraparound services, and instructional quality are linked through shared data systems and collaborative institutional cultures. Chamberlain and Parnell (2022) emphasize that holistic advising requires institution-wide partnerships, not just advising office reform.

**Center equity in design, not just access.** Expanding online access without addressing the digital divide, accessibility, or culturally responsive support can widen rather than narrow equity gaps. Institutions should disaggregate outcomes data by student characteristics, involve students in assessment and design, and invest in supports proportional to need.

**Invest in the advising and support workforce.** High advisor-to-student ratios, staff turnover, and unclear career tracks undermine the sustained relationships that effective advising requires. Adequate staffing, professional development, and institutional recognition are preconditions for the advising models the evidence supports.

**Treat belonging as an institutional responsibility, not a student deficit.** Brady and Gopalan (2025) caution that students' nonbelonging may reasonably reflect inadequate institutional practices. Institutions should examine their own structures, cultures, and policies for barriers to belonging rather than focusing solely on student-level interventions.

**Prepare for shifting educational expectations.** With bachelor's degree expectations declining sharply among high school students (Simone & Christopher, 2025), institutions must reconsider how they communicate value, reduce barriers to entry and completion, and provide clear pathways connecting education to career outcomes.
