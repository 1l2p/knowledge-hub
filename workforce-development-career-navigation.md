## Overview

Workforce development and career navigation encompass the strategies, programs, and systems that connect individuals -- particularly students and early-career workers -- with meaningful employment and upward economic mobility. This domain sits at the intersection of education, employer engagement, labor market dynamics, and individual career management, and it has grown in urgency as the labor market becomes more complex, technology reshapes occupations, and traditional degree pathways prove insufficient for many learners.

Several converging forces shape this landscape. Employer demand for skills-aligned talent is growing while higher education institutions face increasing competition from private providers for workforce training partnerships. Work-based learning models, once confined to the construction trades, are expanding into modern occupations through degree apprenticeships and community college partnerships. Career advising remains fragmented across most institutions, yet emerging AI-powered tools and sector-based intermediaries are beginning to fill gaps. Meanwhile, the COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately disrupted youth employment and accelerated calls for skills-based hiring, durable skills development, and more equitable pathways to economic mobility.

The Axim Strategy Briefing identifies employer-driven pathways, work-based learning, career advising, sector-based training, and better labor market information as critical levers for improving outcomes. Factors such as field of study and regional employer demand are more significant for earnings than school type or degree level, underscoring the importance of aligning education with labor market realities.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Employer-Driven Pathways**: Educational programs designed in partnership with employers to develop students' skills rapidly and align training with actual workforce needs.

- **Work-Based Learning (WBL)**: Learning that integrates academic instruction with real-world work experiences, including internships, apprenticeships, cooperative education, and on-the-job training. WBL involves a tripartite relationship between the educational institution, the employer, and the learner (Voeller, 2023).

- **Degree Apprenticeships**: Programs that integrate registered apprenticeships into degree programs at community colleges or universities, enabling learners to earn wages while pursuing credentials. These address the historical lack of permeability between vocational and academic education in the United States (Voeller, 2023).

- **Pre-Apprenticeships**: Preparatory programs that address access barriers to registered apprenticeship programs, offering wraparound supports, entrance exam preparation, and paid career exploration opportunities (Spaulding et al., 2024).

- **Sector-Based Training**: Employer-led, demand-driven programs involving partnerships among industry, education, workforce development, and community-based organizations to meet the needs of workers and employers in a particular sector.

- **Career Exploration**: Self-regulatory behavior involving the exploration of the environment (occupations, employers, industries) and the self (interests, values, skills) to gather career-related information and support career decision-making (Kleine et al., 2021).

- **Durable Skills**: Enduring, transferable skills that are not job- or role-specific but are valued across all workplaces, including teamwork, communication, ethical behavior, critical thinking, and attention to detail (UpSkill America, 2024).

- **Skills-Based Hiring**: Recruitment practices that emphasize demonstrated competencies rather than degree credentials, aiming to reduce bias and expand access for non-traditional candidates.

- **Career Navigation**: The provision of structured supports -- including advising, mentoring, and navigator models -- to help individuals explore career options, make informed decisions, access employment, and manage career transitions.

- **Digital Badges**: Electronic, metadata-rich symbols of competencies or experiences that provide verifiable evidence of specific skills, serving as an alternative or complement to traditional credentials (Kahle-Piasecki et al., 2024).

## Current State of Research

### Employer Engagement and Partnerships

The relationship between employers and higher education institutions is shifting rapidly. A 2024 UPCEA survey of 507 hiring professionals found that while external training partnerships increased from 54% to 68% between 2022 and 2023, four-year institutions lost market share, declining from 49% to 40% of employer partnerships. Community colleges similarly declined from 45% to 38%. Private providers gained ground: LinkedIn Learning rose from 44% to 52%, and professional associations from 29% to 46% (Etter et al., 2024).

Despite this competitive pressure, employers who do partner with higher education remain highly satisfied. Ninety-eight percent of organizations working with four-year institutions plan to continue those partnerships, citing that programs meet their needs (77%), produce positive employee feedback (71%), and improve job performance (68%). The primary barriers to partnership are cost (37%), lack of real-world application (25%), slow turnaround (22%), and inconsistent content quality (22%). Communication was identified as the most important strategy for overcoming these barriers (Etter et al., 2024).

Employers seek custom programming: 73% reported that their partner institutions create custom content most or all of the time. Strategies most likely to attract non-partnering employers include affordable rates (44%), content with real-world application (35%), consistent quality (33%), and custom programming (31%) (Etter et al., 2024).

The Axim Strategy Briefing highlights community college-employer partnerships as a promising model, citing examples such as Tesla Starts, which partners with 11 community colleges. Intermediary organizations -- nonprofits, private training companies, and industry associations -- are critical for facilitating these connections, particularly in the U.S. where apprenticeship traditions are relatively weak (Voeller, 2023).

### Work-Based Learning and Apprenticeships

Work-based learning encompasses a spectrum of experiences from job shadowing to full registered apprenticeships. Research consistently shows that WBL experiences are more likely to improve employment outcomes when they are paid, when participants can apply concepts learned on the job in the classroom, and when financial support is provided (Spaulding et al., 2024).

**Degree apprenticeships** represent a significant innovation. Voeller (2023) presents two rationales for integrating apprenticeships into degree programs: they enable permeability between vocational and academic credentials, and they equip higher education to prepare students for a globalized knowledge economy. The number of new U.S. apprentices grew 64% between 2012 and 2021, with more than 14,700 new programs created in five years. Community colleges are leading this expansion through initiatives such as Expanding Community College Apprenticeships (ECCA), with over 100 community colleges joining to offer apprenticeships in modern occupations including IT, healthcare, finance, and advanced manufacturing.

Voeller's conceptual framework identifies four interconnected dimensions of quality apprenticeship design: occupational (meaningful workplace engagement), pedagogical (planned learning and assessment), relational (mentoring relationships), and aligned (connection between classroom and workplace learning). A persistent challenge is that employer recruitment remains difficult, as companies must shift from hiring pre-equipped candidates to investing in long-term talent development.

**Pre-apprenticeships** address access barriers to registered programs for young people, women, and people of color. Successful pre-apprenticeship programs offer wraparound supports, prepare trainees for entrance exams, and provide paid exploration opportunities (Spaulding et al., 2024).

**Work-based learning assessment** is an emerging area. Houldsworth et al. (2024) demonstrate how co-designed peer review processes in degree apprenticeship programs can enhance reflective practice and assessment quality. In their case study of a UK master's-level Senior Leadership Apprenticeship, a student-initiated peer review template improved assignment quality, with fewer than 5% of students requiring revision compared to 5-10% in programs relying solely on tutor feedback. The study underscores how apprenticeship learners' professional experience can be leveraged through co-design to create more authentic assessment.

The Axim Strategy Briefing notes that despite increased demand, capacity to scale WBL remains limited. Employer participation is essential but constrained, and there is growing interest in virtual and technology-based solutions to extend reach.

### Career Exploration and Advising

Career exploration is a well-studied construct with strong evidence linking it to positive outcomes. Kleine et al. (2021) conducted a meta-analysis of 109 independent samples (N = 34,969 students) and found that career exploration is positively associated with career decidedness (corrected r = 0.22), perceived employability (corrected r = 0.35), and reduced career self-doubt. Self-efficacy for career exploration and decision-making was the strongest predictor of career exploration behavior (corrected r = 0.52), followed by career-exploratory goals (corrected r = 0.42) and outcome expectations (corrected r = 0.31). Career-related social support, particularly from family, was significantly associated with increased exploration, while perceived barriers diminished it.

These findings have direct implications for practice: interventions that build students' confidence in their ability to explore careers -- particularly among older students closer to the school-to-work transition -- are most likely to increase exploration behavior. Environment exploration (investigating occupations and employers) was more strongly linked to employability than self-exploration (reflecting on one's own interests and values), suggesting that active information-gathering has distinct practical benefits.

The Axim Strategy Briefing characterizes college career services as disjointed and siloed, with a pronounced need for better support during the transition to a first job. Some institutions are integrating career advising with academic programs, and free-standing career support organizations are emerging to help students navigate to high-demand jobs.

**AI in career exploration** is an emerging area with promising early results. Ahn et al. (2024) report on a pilot of an AI-powered Virtual Roadtrip developed by Roadtrip Nation, in which approximately 2,900 students from 45 schools used AI-guided interactions to explore careers through a library of 13,600 professional interview videos. Among the 488 students with complete pre- and post-survey data, agreement that "there are many career options available to me" increased by 12.6 percentage points, confidence in reaching career goals increased by 12.2 points, and ability to identify relatable role models increased by 7.9 points. Students also shifted from mentioning family members as primary role models to identifying a broader range of inspirational figures. The AI guide surfaced 43 unique types of "noise" (pressures and obstacles) that students experience, including discouragement about income potential, pressure to choose "respectable" careers, and self-doubt.

Bankins et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of 104 empirical articles on AI in careers, organized by career stage (school, university-to-work, and established workers). They found that AI is used across all stages for career prediction and guidance, but with important limitations: AI models risk bias by replicating historical inequities, struggle to incorporate subjective factors like aspirations and serendipity, and lack transparency (the "black box" problem). The review identified that AI literacy development is unevenly distributed, with more attention given to school students than to established workers who face technological disruption.

Westman et al. (2021) studied AI adoption for career guidance across Finnish higher education institutions through focus groups, scenario work, and practical trials. Students and staff envisioned AI in roles ranging from information delivery tool to pedagogical companion, with potential for detecting early warning signals, recommending courses and jobs, and creating competence profiles. However, practical trials revealed accuracy challenges: 62% of students found AI-generated job suggestions irrelevant, often due to mismatches in location, field, or career stage. The study proposes a four-mode framework for AI in career guidance: AI as tool (single tasks), assistant (delegated tasks), collaborator (real-time joint work), and coach (interactive virtual career coaching).

### Skills-Based Hiring and Durable Skills

Skills-based hiring is gaining traction as employers and policymakers seek to reduce credentialism and expand access. Spaulding et al. (2024) note that competency-based hiring approaches can reduce bias toward familiar academic and career experiences and prevent algorithmic screening from filtering out qualified candidates who lack traditional degrees.

The UpSkill America (2024) survey of over 550 employers across all 50 states provides detailed evidence on what employers actually value in early-career hires. Employers are split between hiring primarily for role-specific skills (58%) and hiring for attitudes (42%), with those that have removed degree requirements tending to fall into the latter group. The five most valued durable skills are: ethical behavior/integrity (56.3% rating it "extremely important"), teamwork/collaboration (55.3%), attention to detail (52.4%), communication (51.6%), and critical thinking (45.4%). Among attitudes, trustworthiness (60%) and responsibility (60%) dominate.

The study identified five clusters of skills that employers evaluate together rather than in isolation:

1. **Reliability and Execution** (the "non-negotiables"): trustworthiness, attention to detail, responsibility, teamwork, ethical behavior
2. **Problem Solving**: analytical thinking, creative problem solving, critical thinking, communication, customer service
3. **Adaptability and Self-Reflection**: humility, conflict mitigation, metacognition, managing uncertainty
4. **Purpose-Driven Persistence and Inclusivity**: justice, sense of purpose, resilience, inclusion, curiosity
5. **Service-Minded Emotional Intelligence**: service orientation, growth mindset, patience, flexibility, emotional intelligence

A striking finding is that 85% of employers believe durable skills are developed through life and work experience rather than formal instruction. Skills such as empathy, humility, and advocacy -- important for long-term career growth -- are consistently deprioritized at the hiring stage, suggesting a gap between what gets assessed and what enables advancement.

**Digital badges** are emerging as a mechanism to signal skills-based competencies. Kahle-Piasecki et al. (2024) found in a pilot survey of HR professionals that 60% considered digital badges a legitimate credential when evaluating applicants, and 80% believed badges would play an important role in future hiring. However, the study also revealed limited familiarity and concerns about standardization and credibility of badge issuers. A 2022 SHRM survey found that 90% of participants valued alternative credentials, with digital badges requiring a passed exam ranked as a compelling credential alongside registered apprenticeships.

## Challenges and Barriers

**Scaling work-based learning.** Despite strong evidence for the effectiveness of apprenticeships and WBL, scaling remains a persistent challenge. Employer participation is limited by the costs of mentoring and training, the shift from hiring ready-made talent to developing it, and the logistical complexity of coordinating with educational institutions (Voeller, 2023). The Axim Strategy Briefing notes limited capacity to scale WBL despite increased demand.

**Fragmented career advising.** Career services remain disjointed across most institutions, with student-to-counselor ratios averaging 376:1 nationally and reaching 750:1 in some settings (Ahn et al., 2024). Career-transition support, particularly for the move from education to a first job, continues to be problematic.

**Employer-higher education disconnect.** The top barriers to employer partnerships with higher education are cost (37%), lack of real-world application (25%), slow turnaround, inconsistent quality, and infrequent offerings (each ~22%). Four-year institutions are losing ground to private providers who can deliver faster, more customized content (Etter et al., 2024).

**AI limitations and bias.** AI career guidance tools face challenges including data quality and availability, algorithmic bias that may reinforce societal inequalities, lack of transparency and explainability, privacy concerns, and difficulty incorporating subjective factors like aspirations and serendipity (Bankins et al., 2024). Practical trials show that AI recommendations often lack relevance due to insufficient data on location, interests, and career stage (Westman et al., 2021).

**Structural barriers for youth.** Young people, particularly youth of color and those from low-income backgrounds, face compounding barriers including structural racism, poverty, limited access to social capital and networks, exposure to the criminal justice system, and caregiving responsibilities. Resume audit studies show white applicants receive 36% more callbacks than Black applicants and 24% more than Latinx applicants (Spaulding et al., 2024).

**Credential confusion.** The proliferation of digital badges, micro-credentials, and alternative credentials creates challenges around quality assurance, standardization, and employer recognition. Without consistent standards, the value of these credentials remains uncertain (Kahle-Piasecki et al., 2024).

**Durable skills development gap.** While employers overwhelmingly value reliability and execution skills, 85% believe these are developed through experience rather than formal education, creating a misalignment between what education provides and what employers expect (UpSkill America, 2024).

## Best Practices and Recommendations

### For Employer-Education Partnerships
- Designate a single point of contact and invest in ongoing communication with employer partners to reduce friction and build trust (Etter et al., 2024).
- Develop custom programming that directly addresses employer workforce needs while maintaining academic rigor.
- Use intermediary organizations to facilitate connections between employers, educational institutions, and learners, particularly where apprenticeship traditions are weak (Voeller, 2023).

### For Work-Based Learning and Apprenticeships
- Integrate apprenticeships into degree programs to enable permeability between vocational and academic pathways and overcome the "college-for-all" barrier (Voeller, 2023).
- Ensure WBL experiences are paid, connected to classroom learning, and accompanied by financial supports to maximize employment outcomes (Spaulding et al., 2024).
- Provide targeted training for workplace mentors and supervisors, especially those new to hosting apprentices (Voeller, 2023).
- Use co-designed peer review and assessment approaches to leverage apprentices' professional experience and improve learning quality (Houldsworth et al., 2024).
- Expand pre-apprenticeship programs with wraparound supports to address access barriers for underrepresented populations.

### For Career Advising and Exploration
- Build students' career exploration self-efficacy, the strongest predictor of exploration behavior, through targeted interventions, particularly for students approaching labor market transitions (Kleine et al., 2021).
- Integrate career advising with academic programs rather than treating career services as a separate, siloed function.
- Deploy AI-powered career exploration tools that personalize guidance through matching learners with relatable role models and professional stories while surfacing the obstacles students face (Ahn et al., 2024).
- Adopt navigator models -- specialized counselors who help students access and maximize available services and benefits -- with particular attention to disadvantaged youth (Spaulding et al., 2024).

### For Skills Development and Hiring
- Sequence durable skills development by first building the Reliability and Execution core (trustworthiness, attention to detail, responsibility, teamwork, ethical behavior) and then introducing growth-oriented skills for long-term advancement (UpSkill America, 2024).
- Move toward skills-based hiring practices that emphasize demonstrated competencies over credentials to reduce bias and expand access (Spaulding et al., 2024).
- Establish quality standards for digital badges and alternative credentials to ensure they are meaningful, verifiable, and recognized across employers (Kahle-Piasecki et al., 2024).

### For AI in Career Guidance
- Design AI systems as supplements to human guidance rather than replacements, combining AI's scalability with the empathy, trust-building, and nuanced judgment that human counselors provide (Westman et al., 2021).
- Address bias in AI career prediction by proactively diversifying training datasets and auditing outputs for equity (Bankins et al., 2024).
- Build AI literacy across all career stages, including for established workers facing technological disruption (Bankins et al., 2024).

## Implications for Practice

The evidence reviewed across these sources points to several actionable directions for institutions, policymakers, and practitioners:

1. **Labor market alignment is paramount.** Field of study and regional employer demand matter more for earnings than school type or degree level. Institutions should use real-time labor market information -- approximately 60% of two-year colleges already engage private vendors for this purpose -- to inform program design and career advising.

2. **Sector-based models show promise but need scale.** Programs like Year Up, Per Scholas, Project QUEST, and JVS-Boston demonstrate strong outcomes for hard-to-serve populations. Year Up's randomized evaluation showed large positive earnings impacts through seven years of follow-up (Spaulding et al., 2024). The challenge is scaling these intensive models through public systems.

3. **Career academies offer a tested high school model.** Randomized evaluations show positive and sustained earnings impacts eight years after completion, with no negative impact on educational attainment. Elements include industry alignment, work-based learning, college credit opportunities, and peer learning communities.

4. **The employer perspective must inform curriculum.** The mismatch between what employers value (observable, reliability-oriented skills from day one) and what education tends to emphasize (subject matter content) calls for curriculum redesign that deliberately layers foundational workplace competencies with discipline-specific knowledge.

5. **AI is a tool, not a solution.** AI can personalize career guidance at scale, surface student obstacles, and predict career pathways. But current systems face accuracy, bias, and transparency challenges. The most promising applications combine AI curation with human-centered design and maintain human counselors in essential roles.

6. **Equity must be central.** Structural barriers -- racism, poverty, criminal justice involvement, caregiving responsibilities -- shape access to every workforce development strategy discussed here. Programs that acknowledge social determinants of work and provide targeted supports for the most disadvantaged populations are essential for equitable outcomes.
